Tomato Plant Health & Disease Dataset (RAG Optimized)
1. Tomato - Healthy
What it is: A completely normal, thriving tomato plant.

Symptoms & Appearance:

Leaves: Even, solid green color with no yellowing, spots, or curling.

Stems: Strong, upright, and clean.

Flowers & Fruit: Bright yellow flowers that stay attached; smooth, evenly colored fruit with no cracks or dark patches.

Best Conditions:

Warm weather—70 to 85°F during the day, 60 to 70°F at night.

Well-drained soil with a regular supply of moisture.

Plenty of space between plants so air can flow and keep leaves dry.

2. Tomato - Bacterial_spot
What it is: A disease caused by bacteria that leaves ugly, scabby marks on leaves and fruit.

Triggers: High humidity, hot weather (75 to 86°F), and rain splashing water from plant to plant.

Symptoms:

Leaves: Tiny, dark, water-soaked spots (smaller than an eighth of an inch) on the lower leaves. The spots often have a light yellow ring around them.

Fruit: Small, raised, black blisters on green tomatoes. As the fruit grows, these turn into brown, rough, scabby patches.

Treatment & Fixes:

Throw away infected plant parts immediately (the bacteria can live in old plant debris).

Do not water from above (overhead watering). Only work in the garden when plants are completely dry to stop the bacteria from spreading.

Spray copper-based bactericides or organic sprays early in the season to prevent infection.

3. Tomato - Early_blight
What it is: A very common fungal disease that attacks older leaves first and creates a target pattern.

Triggers: Warm, wet weather with lots of dew or frequent rain.

Symptoms:

Leaves: Dark brown or black circular spots starting on the lowest, oldest leaves. The spots look like a bullseye or target board because they have concentric rings inside them. Leaves eventually turn yellow and drop.

Fruit & Stems: Sunken, dark spots with rings form where the stem meets the tomato fruit. Dark, long patches appear on the main stem near the soil line.

Treatment & Fixes:

Rotate your crops—do not plant tomatoes, peppers, or potatoes in the same spot for 2 to 3 years.

Put a thick layer of mulch on the soil to stop fungal spores from splashing up onto the low leaves.

Spray preventative fungicides (like copper or Chlorothalonil) before long rainy periods.

4. Tomato - Late_blight
What it is: A highly destructive water mold disease that spreads incredibly fast in the wind and can kill whole plants in days.

Triggers: Cool, wet, foggy, and damp weather (60 to 70°F).

Symptoms:

Leaves: Large, irregular, dark-green to purplish-black patches that look water-soaked and expand quickly. In damp weather, a white, fuzzy growth appears on the underside of these spots.

Fruit & Stems: Tomatoes get large, firm, greasy-looking, brown patches. Stems turn dark brown and the whole plant collapses.

Treatment & Fixes:

Pull up and completely destroy infected plants (bury or burn them—do not compost them). The disease needs living plant tissue to survive.

Grow hybrid tomato varieties that are bred to resist late blight.

Spray strong preventative copper fungicides immediately if late blight is reported in your area.

5. Tomato - Leaf_Mold
What it is: A fungal disease that targets leaf surfaces, most common in greenhouses and crowded gardens.

Triggers: Very high humidity (above 85%) and mild temperatures.

Symptoms:

Leaves: The tops of older leaves develop pale green or yellow spots. Directly underneath, on the bottom of the leaf, a thick, velvety, olive-green or purplish-brown layer of fungus grows.

Progression: Leaves quickly curl up, wither, and drop off, leaving the fruit bare.

Treatment & Fixes:

Use fans and space plants far apart to keep the humidity below 85%.

Prune off the lower leaves to help air circulate through the bottom of the plant.

Spray copper fungicides or biofungicides as soon as you see the first yellow spots.

6. Tomato - Septoria_leaf_spot
What it is: A fungal disease that causes hundreds of tiny spots on lower leaves, making them drop off.

Triggers: Mild temperatures (60 to 80°F) and lots of rain or overhead sprinkler watering.

Symptoms:

Leaves: Tons of tiny, round spots (about an eighth of an inch wide) on lower leaves. The spots have dark brown edges and light gray or tan centers.

Unique Sign: If you look closely at the tan centers, you can see tiny black dots that look like pimples. Leaves turn yellow and drop off from the bottom of the plant upward.

Treatment & Fixes:

Wait 3 years before planting tomatoes in the same soil. Keep the garden clear of weeds like horsenettle.

Water at the base of the plant (drip irrigation) to keep leaves dry.

Spray protectant fungicides at the very first sign of leaf spotting.

7. Tomato - Target_Spot
What it is: A fungal disease that causes small, sharply ringed circles on both leaves and fruit.

Triggers: Warm weather (68 to 90°F), high humidity, and wet leaves.

Symptoms:

Leaves: Small, water-soaked dots on the upper leaf surface that grow into circles with very fine, sharp concentric rings inside them.

Fruit: Small, slightly sunken, round pits on the tomato fruit. As the disease gets worse, the centers of these pits split and crack open.

Treatment & Fixes:

Stake and prune plants so they dry out quickly after rain.

Switch between different types of fungicides so the fungus doesn't become immune (resistant) to the spray.

8. Tomato - Tomato_mosaic_virus
What it is: A highly contagious virus that is spread purely by touch and stunts plant growth.

Triggers: Spread by hands, tools, clothing, and pruning shears. It can live for years in old plant roots, soil, and seeds.

Symptoms:

Leaves: A green and yellow mosaic pattern (mottled look) on the leaves. Leaves may look twisted, wrinkled, or turn into thin, stringy shapes like shoelaces.

Plant & Fruit: The whole plant stops growing and looks stunted. Tomatoes ripen unevenly and get brown patches inside the fruit walls.

Treatment & Fixes:

Wash your hands with soap and dip tools in a disinfectant (like a 20% non-fat dry milk solution) before moving to a new plant.

Buy certified virus-free seeds. If a plant gets infected, pull it up and throw it away immediately—do not compost it.

9. Tomato - Tomato_Yellow_Leaf_Curl_Virus
What it is: A destructive virus that stops fruit production and turns plants into dense, yellow bushes.

Triggers: Spread entirely by a tiny insect called the Silverleaf Whitefly. It cannot be spread by touch.

Symptoms:

Leaves: The edges of the leaves roll sharply upward and inward. Leaves stay small, crumpled, and turn bright yellow between the green veins.

Plant: The plant stops growing taller, becoming a tight, bushy mound that looks like a miniature bonsai or a head of broccoli. Flowers drop off, so no tomatoes grow.

Treatment & Fixes:

Control whiteflies using insecticidal soaps, neem oil, or reflective silver mulches that scare the bugs away.

Plant hybrid tomato varieties that are naturally resistant to the virus.

Pull up and destroy sick plants immediately so whiteflies can't carry the virus from them to healthy plants.

10. Tomato - Two-spotted_spider_mite
What it is: Tiny, spider-like pests that suck the sap out of leaves, creating fine webbing.

Triggers: Hot, dry, and dusty weather. Their population can explode in just a few days.

Symptoms:

Leaves: Thousands of tiny white or yellow dots (stippling) across the top of the leaves where the mites are feeding.

Webbing: Fine, silk-like spider webs covering the undersides of leaves and the growing tips of the plant. Leaves eventually turn bronze, dry up, and fall off.

Treatment & Fixes:

Spray the plants down with a strong blast of water to break their webs and knock them off. Keep pathways damp to reduce dust.

Release beneficial bugs like predatory mites, which hunt and eat spider mites.

Avoid harsh chemical bug sprays (like pyrethroids), because they kill the good bugs and actually cause spider mite numbers to skyrocket. Use horticultural oils instead.
